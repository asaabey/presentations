================================================================================
Perioperative medication optimisation: five GP decisions
================================================================================

--- [1] SLIDE: Perioperative medication optimisation: five GP decisions  (intro)
A 15-minute guide to chronic disease medicines before elective surgery.
- GLP-1 medicines, anticoagulants and rheumatoid arthritis biologics
- SGLT2 inhibitors and ACE inhibitors/ARBs
- Leave with a written last dose, restart and review plan
Australian practice focus • Guidance checked 17 November 2026
Agree the plan with the procedural and anaesthetic teams.

--- [2] SLIDE: Case 1: read the clues  (q-glp1-clues)
Helen, 58, takes weekly semaglutide for type 2 diabetes. She has been on a
stable dose for six months and has no gastrointestinal symptoms. She is booked
for elective laparoscopic surgery under general anaesthesia in two weeks.

--- [3] Q1  (q-glp1) ----------------------------------------------------------
Helen, 58, takes weekly semaglutide for type 2 diabetes. She has been on a
stable dose for six months and has no gastrointestinal symptoms. She is booked
for elective laparoscopic surgery under general anaesthesia in two weeks.

Under current Australian guidance, which plan is most appropriate?

  A. Omit semaglutide for one week, then use ordinary fasting instructions alone
* B. Continue semaglutide; arrange the recommended clear-fluid diet and
     anaesthetic fasting plan
  C. Continue semaglutide; ordinary fasting alone is enough because she has no
     symptoms
  D. Stop semaglutide for four weeks and restart automatically on the day after
     surgery

  ANSWER: B
  EXPLANATION:
    Australian guidance recommends continuing GLP-1 and dual GLP-1/GIP
    medicines, with a 24-hour clear-fluid diet before the procedure, followed by
    the anaesthetic fasting instructions. Lack of symptoms does not exclude
    retained gastric contents.
    - ANZCA clarifies: water only in the final six hours; nothing by mouth in
      the final two hours. Obtain the hospital's exact timings.
    - A routine one-week omission does not reliably remove the gastric-emptying
      risk; four weeks is not the routine recommendation.
    - If the diet was not followed, or symptoms or recent dose escalation raise
      concern, contact anaesthesia for assessment and a proceeding/postponement
      plan.
    GP action: reconcile other diabetes medicines and glucose monitoring during
    the diet. (Ref 1)

--- [4] SLIDE: Case 2: read the clues  (q-apixaban-clues)
David, 72, takes apixaban 5 mg twice daily for atrial fibrillation. His CrCl is
70 mL/min, with no recent stroke or venous thromboembolism. He is having a high-
bleeding-risk abdominal operation on Friday morning. General anaesthesia is
planned, without spinal or epidural anaesthesia.

--- [5] Q2  (q-apixaban) ------------------------------------------------------
David, 72, takes apixaban 5 mg twice daily for atrial fibrillation. His CrCl is
70 mL/min, with no recent stroke or venous thromboembolism. He is having a high-
bleeding-risk abdominal operation on Friday morning. General anaesthesia is
planned, without spinal or epidural anaesthesia.

Which apixaban plan should the GP confirm with the surgical team?

  A. Omit only Friday morning's dose and resume the full dose on Friday evening
  B. Omit Wednesday to Friday and use therapeutic-dose enoxaparin as a bridge
  C. Take the last dose Wednesday evening; omit Thursday and Friday; resume the
     full dose on Saturday
* D. Take the last dose Tuesday evening; omit Wednesday to Friday; plan
     resumption after haemostasis

  ANSWER: D
  EXPLANATION:
    For this standard-dose apixaban case, omit the two days before and the day
    of surgery: last dose Tuesday evening for Friday surgery. Therapeutic-dose
    heparin bridging is not indicated.
    - Resume therapeutic apixaban usually 48–72 hours after high-bleeding-risk
      surgery, once haemostasis and oral intake permit; the surgical team
      confirms timing.
    - Postoperative prophylactic anticoagulation, when safe, is a separate
      decision from therapeutic bridging.
    - A morning-only omission is too short. The Wednesday-last-dose option uses
      the shorter interruption for lower bleeding risk and restarts too early
      for this case.
    This schedule is not transferable to every anticoagulant: renal impairment,
    dabigatran, spinal/epidural procedures and very high thrombotic risk need a
    specific plan. (Ref 2)

--- [6] SLIDE: Case 3: read the clues  (q-biologic-clues)
Ruth, 61, has rheumatoid arthritis controlled with adalimumab every two weeks
and weekly methotrexate. Renal function and blood counts are normal. She is
awaiting total knee replacement. After discussing infection and flare risks, her
rheumatologist and surgeon have agreed to interrupt adalimumab for one dosing
interval.

--- [7] Q3  (q-biologic) ------------------------------------------------------
Ruth, 61, has rheumatoid arthritis controlled with adalimumab every two weeks
and weekly methotrexate. Renal function and blood counts are normal. She is
awaiting total knee replacement. After discussing infection and flare risks, her
rheumatologist and surgeon have agreed to interrupt adalimumab for one dosing
interval.

Which plan best implements that decision?

* A. Continue methotrexate; miss the next adalimumab dose and arrange surgery in
     week 3 after the last injection
  B. Stop both medicines for six weeks before surgery and six weeks afterwards
  C. Continue methotrexate; omit adalimumab only if an injection falls on the
     day of surgery
  D. Miss the next adalimumab dose, then restart it on day 3 regardless of wound
     healing

  ANSWER: A
  EXPLANATION:
    When the team elects to withhold fortnightly adalimumab, miss one dose;
    operate in week 3 after the last injection. Methotrexate is usually
    continued in this uncomplicated case.
    - Restart adalimumab only when the wound is healing, sutures/staples are out
      and there is no infection, often around 14 days; obtain team approval.
    - Stopping everything for weeks increases flare risk; omitting only the
      surgery-day dose does not implement the agreed interval.
    Guidance differs: Australian recommendations do not routinely stop all
    biologics, but consider interruption for higher infection risk or
    consequences. ACR/AAHKS favours withholding biologics for hip/knee
    replacement. The case explicitly uses an agreed interruption plan. (Refs
    3–4)

Michael, 66, takes morning empagliflozin for type 2 diabetes and heart failure.
Elective major abdominal surgery is booked for Friday, with an inpatient stay.
At Monday's GP visit, he asks whether he should take empagliflozin until

--- [9] Q4  (q-sglt2) ---------------------------------------------------------
Michael, 66, takes morning empagliflozin for type 2 diabetes and heart failure.
Elective major abdominal surgery is booked for Friday, with an inpatient stay.
At Monday's GP visit, he asks whether he should take empagliflozin until
Thursday because his glucose readings are normal.

Which is the best plan for this Australian inpatient-surgery scenario?

  A. Continue until Thursday because normal glucose excludes ketoacidosis risk
  B. Omit Friday's dose only and restart on Saturday even if still fasting
* C. Last dose Tuesday morning; omit Wednesday, Thursday and Friday; agree
     monitoring and restart criteria
  D. Omit Wednesday to Friday, then restart as soon as glucose rises even if
     still fasting

  ANSWER: C
  EXPLANATION:
    ADS–ANZCA guidance for surgery with an inpatient stay is to omit SGLT2
    inhibitors for two days before and the day of surgery. In this morning-dose
    example, Tuesday is the last dose before Friday surgery.
    - Fasting and surgical stress can cause ketoacidosis despite a near-normal
      glucose. Nausea, abdominal pain, rapid breathing or unexplained illness
      require urgent assessment with blood ketones and acid–base testing.
    - Restart only when eating and drinking normally, clinically stable and free
      of ketosis, with the treating team's agreement.
    - Heart failure benefit does not remove this perioperative risk; a rising
      glucose alone is not a safe restart criterion.
    Confirm local instructions: some protocols use longer holds, particularly
    for ertugliflozin. (Ref 5)

--- [10] SLIDE: Case 5: read the clues  (q-ace-arb-clues)
Anne, 69, takes perindopril solely for hypertension. BP is 126/74 mmHg; she has
no heart failure. She is preparing for elective major vascular surgery. Her pre-
anaesthetic medication plan has not yet been completed.

--- [11] Q5  (q-ace-arb) -------------------------------------------------------
Anne, 69, takes perindopril solely for hypertension. BP is 126/74 mmHg; she has
no heart failure. She is preparing for elective major vascular surgery. Her pre-
anaesthetic medication plan has not yet been completed.

Which recommendation is most appropriate to discuss with anaesthesia?

  A. Continue perindopril without seeking anaesthetic advice because her blood
     pressure is well controlled
* B. Consider omitting perindopril 24 hours beforehand to limit hypotension;
     document when to restart
  C. Withhold perindopril and every other antihypertensive for 48 hours before
     surgery
  D. Use the same mandatory ACE inhibitor hold for all patients with reduced-
     ejection-fraction heart failure

  ANSWER: B
  EXPLANATION:
    For controlled hypertension before elevated-risk noncardiac surgery,
    omission of an ACE inhibitor or ARB 24 hours beforehand may reduce
    intraoperative hypotension. It is an option to agree with anaesthesia, not a
    universal rule.
    - If prescribed for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction,
      continuation may be reasonable; indication changes the balance.
    - Trials have not shown that routine withholding improves major
      postoperative outcomes. Stopping every antihypertensive is inappropriate;
      a controlled BP does not remove the need for an agreed plan.
    - If withheld, document reassessment and restart once blood pressure, volume
      status, renal function and potassium allow. Prevent accidental long-term
      omission.
    GP action: record why each medicine is prescribed before deciding whether to
    interrupt it. (Ref 6)

--- [12] SLIDE: The GP handover: make every instruction actionable  (checklist)
1. Reconcile medicines, indication, renal function and procedure/anaesthetic
   risk.
2. Write the last dose date and time; include fasting and glucose instructions.
3. Name the clinician responsible for restart and review.
4. State restart conditions: bleeding, oral intake, wound, BP and kidney
   function.
5. Give the patient and procedural team the same written plan.
Every temporary hold needs a restart plan.
