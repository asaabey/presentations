================================================================================
Psychiatric pharmacotherapy in kidney disease and transplantation
================================================================================

--- [1] SLIDE: Psychiatric pharmacotherapy in kidney disease and transplantation  (intro)
A practical, case-based session for general practice.

By the end you should be able to:
1. Choose a reasonable first-line antidepressant in CKD stages 3–5, including
   dialysis
2. Recognise psychotropics that accumulate in renal impairment
3. Manage lithium safely, including during acute illness
4. Spot important psychotropic–immunosuppressant interactions
5. Ask: is this really a psychiatric illness?

--- [2] SLIDE: Three different questions for every drug  (three-issues)
1. Clearance: is it renally cleared? Does the dose need to change?
2. Accumulation: do active drug or metabolites build up and cause toxicity?
3. Nephrotoxicity: does it damage the kidney?

A drug can be completely non-nephrotoxic and still cause severe encephalopathy
as kidney function falls.

--- [3] SLIDE: Read the case: key clues  (q-ad-ckd4-clues)
Margaret, 64, has type 2 diabetes and CKD stage 4 (eGFR 22). She has moderate
depression and would like to try medication alongside counselling.

--- [4] Q1  (q-ad-ckd4) -------------------------------------------------------
Margaret, 64, has type 2 diabetes and CKD stage 4 (eGFR 22). She has moderate
depression and would like to try medication alongside counselling.

Which is the most reasonable first choice?

  A. Desvenlafaxine 100 mg daily
* B. Sertraline 25 mg daily, increasing to 50 mg
  C. Duloxetine 60 mg daily
  D. Amitriptyline 25 mg at night

  ANSWER: B
  EXPLANATION:
    Sertraline is hepatically metabolised, needs no formal renal dose
    adjustment, and has relatively low interaction potential, so it is a common
    first choice in advanced CKD. Start low and titrate slowly.

    - Desvenlafaxine is largely renally cleared. At CrCl <30 the dose must be
      reduced substantially (no more than 50 mg every second day); 100 mg daily
      is too much.
    - Duloxetine exposure rises markedly in severe renal impairment. Many
      guidelines advise avoiding it below CrCl 30, and 60 mg would be
      inappropriate here.
    - Amitriptyline brings anticholinergic effects, orthostatic hypotension,
      falls and QT prolongation, all worse in CKD.

--- [5] SLIDE: Antidepressants in CKD: the table to keep  (ad-table)

    Drug           | Renal considerations                  | GP message
    ---------------+---------------------------------------+--------------------------------------------------------------------
    Sertraline     | No formal renal adjustment            | Often a useful first choice, including advanced CKD and dialysis
    Escitalopram   | Caution at CrCl <30                   | Dose-related QT prolongation; check electrolytes and other QT drugs
    Fluoxetine     | Little renal adjustment               | Very long half-life; significant CYP interactions
    Venlafaxine    | Drug and active metabolite accumulate | Reduce dose 25–50% in CKD; about 50% on dialysis
    Desvenlafaxine | Mainly renally cleared                | CrCl <30 or dialysis: max 50 mg every second day
    Duloxetine     | Exposure rises at CrCl <30            | Generally avoid at CrCl <30; recommendations vary by formulation
    Mirtazapine    | Clearance falls 30–50%                | Start 7.5 mg; helps sleep and appetite; watch sedation and falls
    TCAs           | Metabolites accumulate                | Avoid for depression: anticholinergic, falls, QT

--- [6] SLIDE: A note on duloxetine  (duloxetine-nuance)
- eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m²: generally avoid duloxetine because exposure rises in
  severe renal impairment
- For some formulations, 30 mg daily is recommended in severe renal impairment

Treat this as a formulation-specific prescribing decision, not a universal ban.
If you use it, use the lowest dose and monitor closely.

--- [7] SLIDE: Read the case: key clues  (q-ssri-na-clues)
Joan, 79, has CKD stage 3b and persistent depression despite psychological
treatment. Six months ago, she was admitted with confusion and sodium 121 mmol/L
shortly after starting escitalopram while taking a thiazide. Both medicines were
stopped, and her sodium returned to normal. Her current sodium is 138 mmol/L.

--- [8] Q2  (q-ssri-na) -------------------------------------------------------
Joan, 79, has CKD stage 3b and persistent depression despite psychological
treatment. Six months ago, she was admitted with confusion and sodium 121 mmol/L
shortly after starting escitalopram while taking a thiazide. Both medicines were
stopped, and her sodium returned to normal. Her current sodium is 138 mmol/L.

She would now like to try another antidepressant. Which is the most appropriate
option given her previous adverse reaction?

  A. Sertraline
  B. Venlafaxine
  C. Escitalopram at a lower dose
* D. Mirtazapine

  ANSWER: D
  EXPLANATION:
    Mirtazapine is the most appropriate option here because it carries a lower,
    though not zero, risk of hyponatraemia than SSRIs and SNRIs. Her previous
    severe episode should influence the choice even though her sodium is now
    normal and the thiazide has been stopped.

    - Sertraline has favourable renal dosing, but switching to another SSRI does
      not remove the risk of recurrent hyponatraemia.
    - Venlafaxine also carries a risk of hyponatraemia and requires renal dose
      adjustment.
    - Escitalopram at a lower dose re-exposes her to the suspected causative
      drug; a lower dose does not reliably prevent recurrence.

    Mirtazapine clearance is reduced in CKD: start cautiously, titrate slowly,
    and monitor sedation and falls. Check sodium at baseline and early after
    starting treatment, with repeat monitoring guided by her risk and clinical
    course. New confusion or unsteadiness warrants prompt assessment and a
    sodium check.

--- [9] SLIDE: Antidepressants: hyponatraemia risk  (ad-hyponatraemia)
Pooled event rates, not a ranking: populations, follow-up and sodium thresholds
varied substantially. These are not CKD-specific or annual risks; wide
confidence intervals limit comparisons between individual drugs.

Direct comparisons support lower risk with mirtazapine than SSRIs, but risk is
not zero (OR 0.61; 95% CI 0.39–0.96).

Source: [Gheysens T et al. European Psychiatry 2024;67:e20, Tables
3–4](https://doi.org/10.1192/j.eurpsy.2024.11). Any hyponatraemia: Na <135
mmol/L or a documented diagnosis; some studies only reported Na <130.

    Agent               | Pooled event rate (95% CI)                       | Studies
    --------------------+--------------------------------------------------+--------
    Sertraline          | 4.59% (0.63–26.81%)                              | 7
    Escitalopram        | 3.21% (0.33–24.86%)                              | 6
    Fluoxetine          | 3.33% (1.09–9.71%)                               | 12
    Citalopram          | 0.95% (0.04–20.53%)                              | 3
    Paroxetine          | 5.42% (1.38–18.98%)                              | 11
    Fluvoxamine         | 4.98% (3.79–6.52%)                               | 4
    Venlafaxine         | 6.99% (1.05–34.61%)                              | 8
    Desvenlafaxine      | No pooled agent-specific estimate in this review | —
    Duloxetine          | 3.86% (0.07–71.04%)                              | 3
    Mirtazapine         | 1.02% (0.25–4.02%)                               | 8
    Amitriptyline (TCA) | 2.05% (0.01–84.30%)                              | 3

--- [10] SLIDE: SSRIs are not free of kidney-related problems  (ssri-harms)
- Hyponatraemia: first weeks; older age, thiazides, low body weight
- QT prolongation: citalopram and escitalopram are dose-related. In dialysis
  patients, potassium, calcium and magnesium shifts add risk
- Serotonin toxicity: watch tramadol, tapentadol, linezolid and ondansetron
  combinations
- Falls and fractures: on top of CKD–mineral bone disease

--- [11] SLIDE: Read the case: key clues  (q-li-decline-clues)
Peter, 56, has bipolar I disorder and has been well on lithium for 22 years.
Level 0.7 mmol/L.

- eGFR has fallen from 78 to 46 over 10 years
- He passes about 4 L of urine a day
- Urine ACR is normal

--- [12] Q3  (q-li-decline) ----------------------------------------------------
Peter, 56, has bipolar I disorder and has been well on lithium for 22 years.
Level 0.7 mmol/L.

- eGFR has fallen from 78 to 46 over 10 years
- He passes about 4 L of urine a day
- Urine ACR is normal

What is the best next step?

* A. Continue lithium for now and arrange specialist review of renal function
     and polyuria
  B. Reduce the lithium dose and repeat renal function in three months
  C. Arrange a gradual switch to another mood stabiliser because eGFR is below
     60
  D. Continue the current regimen with routine monitoring because the lithium
     level is therapeutic

  ANSWER: A
  EXPLANATION:
    This is classic lithium-associated CKD (chronic tubulointerstitial
    nephropathy) with nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Decisions need to be
    shared between him, his psychiatrist and nephrology.

    - Reducing the dose alone does not address the cause of the renal decline or
      polyuria; waiting three months delays assessment.
    - An eGFR below 60 alone does not mandate switching. Weigh the renal
      trajectory against lithium’s benefit and the risks of relapse.
    - A therapeutic lithium level does not exclude chronic renal adverse effects
      or justify routine monitoring alone.

    The best window for the decision to continue or switch is early, while eGFR
    is still moderately preserved.

--- [13] SLIDE: Lithium and the kidney  (li-kidney)
- Nephrogenic DI: common (polyuria in up to about 1 in 3). Lithium enters
  collecting-duct cells through ENaC and blunts the response to ADH
- Chronic tubulointerstitial nephropathy: slow eGFR decline over years to
  decades; often bland urine and microcysts on imaging
- Progression to kidney failure: uncommon, but real after long exposure
- Also: hypercalcaemia (hyperparathyroidism) and hypothyroidism
- Acute toxicity episodes add to long-term kidney damage

--- [14] SLIDE: Monitoring and when to involve nephrology  (li-monitor)
Every 3–6 months: lithium level (12 h post-dose), U&E/eGFR, calcium and TSH.
Check more often in older people and those with CKD.

Target for most adults is 0.6–0.8 mmol/L; use the lower end in older people.

Involve nephrology when:
- eGFR is <45, or falling steadily (for example, >5 mL/min/1.73m² a year)
- There is albuminuria or haematuria without another explanation
- Polyuria or nocturia is troublesome, or sodium is abnormal
- Calcium is raised

--- [15] SLIDE: Lithium during acute illness  (li-sick-days)
Vomiting, diarrhoea and poor fluid intake can precipitate lithium toxicity and
AKI, especially with an ACE inhibitor, ARB, diuretic or NSAID.

- Withhold lithium during significant dehydrating illness and arrange same-day
  clinical review, lithium level and renal function/electrolytes
- Review other medicines that may need temporary withholding during dehydration
- Coarse tremor, ataxia, slurred speech, confusion, myoclonus or seizures
  require emergency assessment
- Restart lithium with a clinician-agreed plan once hydration and renal function
  are satisfactory; recheck levels as advised

--- [16] SLIDE: Drugs that move lithium levels  (li-interactions)
Recheck the lithium level about 5–7 days after starting, stopping or changing
any of these.

    Drug                  | Effect on lithium       | Action
    ----------------------+-------------------------+------------------------------------------------------
    Thiazide diuretics    | ↑ about 25–40%          | Avoid if possible; reduce dose and monitor if needed
    ACE inhibitors / ARBs | ↑ (variable)            | Often indicated in CKD: start, then recheck the level
    NSAIDs (incl. COX-2)  | ↑                       | Avoid; warn about over-the-counter use
    Loop diuretics        | ↑ (smaller effect)      | Monitor
    SGLT2 inhibitors      | ↓ (increased excretion) | Monitor for loss of effect
    Amlodipine            | Minimal                 | Reasonable antihypertensive choice

--- [17] SLIDE: Read the case: key clues  (q-valproate-free-clues)
Ravi, 62, takes valproate for bipolar disorder. He has CKD stage 5 and albumin
25 g/L. He develops new drowsiness and tremor. His total valproate concentration
is within the laboratory therapeutic range.

--- [18] Q4  (q-valproate-free) ------------------------------------------------
Ravi, 62, takes valproate for bipolar disorder. He has CKD stage 5 and albumin
25 g/L. He develops new drowsiness and tremor. His total valproate concentration
is within the laboratory therapeutic range.

Which is the best interpretation?

  A. The total level excludes valproate toxicity
  B. Low albumin means the dose should be increased
* C. A normal total level does not exclude toxicity: the free valproate fraction
     may be increased
  D. Valproate is eliminated entirely unchanged by the kidneys

  ANSWER: C
  EXPLANATION:
    Renal failure and low albumin reduce valproate protein binding. The
    pharmacologically active free fraction may rise even when the total
    concentration appears therapeutic.

    Assess urgently for medication toxicity and other causes of altered
    consciousness. Discuss a free valproate concentration where available; check
    ammonia when valproate-associated encephalopathy is suspected. Do not
    increase the dose simply to chase a total concentration.


    Reference: [Valproate: renal disease and protein binding](https://dailymed.n
    lm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4b619f73-44e5-4a99-b425-
    a96cb2228c87).

--- [20] SLIDE: Read the case: key clues  (q-antipsych-clues)
Daniel, 48, has schizophrenia and CKD stage 4 (CrCl 25 mL/min). At a routine GP
appointment, you review a letter from his psychiatrist recommending a new oral
antipsychotic.

--- [21] Q5  (q-antipsych) -----------------------------------------------------
Daniel, 48, has schizophrenia and CKD stage 4 (CrCl 25 mL/min). At a routine GP
appointment, you review a letter from his psychiatrist recommending a new oral
antipsychotic.

Which of the following would most concern you about accumulation in renal
impairment and prompt you to check the prescribed dose?

  A. Olanzapine
* B. Paliperidone
  C. Quetiapine
  D. Haloperidol

  ANSWER: B
  EXPLANATION:
    Paliperidone (the active metabolite of risperidone) is largely excreted
    unchanged by the kidney. At CrCl 10–50 the oral dose is reduced to 1.5 mg
    daily, and it is not recommended below CrCl 10. Long-acting injectable doses
    also need adjustment.

    Risperidone needs a similar caution: reduce the dose by about 50%.

    Olanzapine, quetiapine and haloperidol are hepatically metabolised and
    usually need no renal adjustment. Their problems in CKD are sedation,
    orthostasis, metabolic effects and QT prolongation.

--- [22] SLIDE: Antipsychotics and sedatives in CKD  (sedatives)

    Drug                 | Main concern in CKD                      | Priority
    ---------------------+------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------
    Paliperidone         | Substantial renal elimination            | High
    Risperidone          | Active moiety accumulates                | High
    Quetiapine           | Sedation, orthostasis, QT                | Moderate
    Olanzapine           | Metabolic effects, sedation              | Moderate
    Diazepam             | Long half-life, active metabolites       | High: avoid
    Oxazepam / temazepam | No active metabolites; still cause falls | Prefer if a benzodiazepine is needed
    Zolpidem / zopiclone | Falls, confusion                         | Short courses only

--- [23] SLIDE: Read the case: key clues  (q-insomnia-clues)
Anne, 74, has CKD stage 4 (CrCl 24 mL/min) and severe, short-term insomnia after
a bereavement. Assessment has not identified depression, sleep apnoea, restless
legs or uncontrolled pain/itch. She takes no opioids, drinks no alcohol and has
no history of hypnotic dependence. Non-drug sleep strategies have started.

--- [24] Q6  (q-insomnia) ------------------------------------------------------
Anne, 74, has CKD stage 4 (CrCl 24 mL/min) and severe, short-term insomnia after
a bereavement. Assessment has not identified depression, sleep apnoea, restless
legs or uncontrolled pain/itch. She takes no opioids, drinks no alcohol and has
no history of hypnotic dependence. Non-drug sleep strategies have started.

After discussing risks, she and her GP agree on a brief hypnotic trial. Which
prescription is most appropriate?

* A. Zopiclone 3.75 mg at night for up to 7 days, with a review and stop plan
  B. Diazepam 5 mg at night for up to 7 days, with a review and stop plan
  C. Temazepam 10 mg at night for 4 weeks before review
  D. Zopiclone 7.5 mg at night for 4 weeks before review

  ANSWER: A
  EXPLANATION:
    Zopiclone 3.75 mg for a brief trial is the best of these options. The
    recommended starting dose in renal impairment is 3.75 mg, for short-term
    treatment (7–14 days). Review within a week, use the lowest effective dose
    and agree when to stop.

    - Diazepam has a long half-life and active metabolites, increasing concern
      about prolonged sedation in an older person.
    - Temazepam has no active metabolites and can be an alternative when a
      benzodiazepine is needed. The problem with this option is a four-week
      supply before review, not a claim that temazepam is always unsuitable in
      CKD.
    - Zopiclone 7.5 mg is above the recommended starting dose for this patient;
      delaying review for four weeks is also inappropriate.

    Zopiclone acts at the benzodiazepine receptor and still carries risks of
    falls, next-day impairment, dependence and rebound insomnia. Complex sleep
    behaviours are another important adverse effect. Do not combine it with a
    benzodiazepine, alcohol or other sedatives.


    Continue non-drug treatment; persistent insomnia warrants reassessment and
    CBT-I.

    Reference: [Zopiclone](https://www.safetyandquality.gov.au/medicine-
    finder/zopiclone-gh).

--- [25] SLIDE: Stacking risk in dialysis patients  (polypharmacy)
A typical haemodialysis medication list might include:
- Escitalopram, quetiapine and ondansetron, plus
- post-dialysis hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia

That adds up to QT stacking, and the risk is cumulative.

- Get a baseline ECG when combining QT-prolonging drugs
- Minimise anticholinergic burden (falls, delirium, constipation)
- Review sedatives at every opportunity

--- [26] SLIDE: Calcineurin inhibitors: one mechanism, two dangers  (tx-mechanism)
Tacrolimus and ciclosporin are metabolised by CYP3A4 and transported by
P-glycoprotein, and both have a narrow therapeutic range.

Either direction is dangerous: too much causes toxicity, too little causes
rejection.

                          | Inhibitor added                           | Inducer added
    ----------------------+-------------------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------
    Tacrolimus metabolism | ↓                                         | ↑
    Tacrolimus level      | ↑                                         | ↓
    Clinical result       | AKI, tremor, neurotoxicity, hyperkalaemia | Under-immunosuppression, rejection, graft loss
    When the drug stops   | Level falls: rejection risk               | Level rises: toxicity risk

--- [28] Q7  (q-tx-cbz) --------------------------------------------------------
Tom, 45, is 4 years post kidney transplant on tacrolimus, with stable creatinine
around 120 µmol/L. A psychiatrist starts carbamazepine for bipolar disorder.

Three weeks later his creatinine is 165 µmol/L.

What is the most likely mechanism?

  A. CYP3A4 inhibition raising tacrolimus, causing nephrotoxicity
  B. Direct carbamazepine nephrotoxicity
  C. Carbamazepine-induced SIADH
* D. CYP3A4 induction lowering tacrolimus, leading to acute rejection

  ANSWER: D
  EXPLANATION:
    Carbamazepine is a strong enzyme inducer. Induction builds up over 2–3
    weeks, tacrolimus levels fall, and rejection follows. This needs an urgent
    tacrolimus trough level and same-day contact with the transplant unit.

    - Inhibition is the opposite mechanism; carbamazepine induces.
    - Carbamazepine is not significantly nephrotoxic.
    - SIADH causes hyponatraemia, not a creatinine rise.

    Phenytoin, phenobarbital, rifampicin and St John's wort behave similarly.
    Oxcarbazepine is a weaker inducer but still needs specialist review and
    monitoring.

--- [29] SLIDE: Psychotropic–immunosuppressant interactions  (tx-table)

    Drug           | Interaction                    | Practical action
    ---------------+--------------------------------+---------------------------------------------
    Fluoxetine     | Possible ↑ CNI                 | Check interactions; monitor levels
    Sertraline     | Low potential                  | Often reasonable; still review
    Escitalopram   | Limited CYP effect; QT         | ECG, electrolytes, other QT drugs
    Carbamazepine  | Strong induction: ↓↓ CNI       | Avoid where possible: rejection risk
    Oxcarbazepine  | Induction: ↓ CNI               | Specialist review and monitoring
    St John's wort | Strong induction: ↓↓ CNI       | Avoid
    Quetiapine     | CYP3A4 substrate; QT, sedation | Monitor, especially with azoles or diltiazem

--- [30] SLIDE: What to do in practice  (tx-actions)
- Contact the transplant team when starting, stopping or changing an interacting
  psychotropic
- Tacrolimus trough and creatinine at about 5–7 days, then as advised
- Remember the reversal: when the interacting drug stops, levels swing back
- Ciclosporin is itself a CYP3A4 inhibitor and can raise levels of some
  psychotropics
- Never adjust immunosuppression yourself. Coordinate it

--- [33] Q8  (q-memantine) -----------------------------------------------------
George, 80, has Alzheimer disease and CKD stage 4. His estimated CrCl is 22
mL/min. A letter proposes increasing immediate-release memantine from 5 mg twice
daily to 10 mg twice daily.

What should the GP do?

  A. Increase as proposed because cognitive medicines do not need renal
     adjustment
  B. Stop all cognitive treatment because CKD stage 4 is an absolute
     contraindication
* C. Query the increase: severe renal impairment requires a lower target dose
  D. Double the dose because renal impairment reduces absorption

  ANSWER: C
  EXPLANATION:
    Memantine exposure increases in renal impairment. For immediate-release
    tablets, the recommended target dose is 5 mg twice daily at CrCl 5–29
    mL/min. Do not routinely increase this patient to 10 mg twice daily.

    Confirm the recommendation for the prescribed formulation. Monitor
    dizziness, confusion and falls; new cognitive deterioration also needs
    assessment for delirium.


    Reference: [Memantine, immediate release](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/daily
    med/drugInfo.cfm?setid=30b4d5c4-0a31-c385-e063-6394a90adbce).

--- [35] Q9  (q-acamprosate) ---------------------------------------------------
Leanne, 54, has alcohol dependence and CKD stage 4, with estimated CrCl 24
mL/min. After completing withdrawal treatment she asks about acamprosate to help
maintain abstinence.

What is the best next step?

* A. Avoid acamprosate at this kidney function and discuss an alternative
     relapse-prevention plan
  B. Start the usual full dose because acamprosate is not hepatically
     metabolised
  C. Start half the usual dose without further review
  D. Use acamprosate to treat acute alcohol withdrawal

  ANSWER: A
  EXPLANATION:
    Acamprosate is renally eliminated. It is contraindicated at CrCl ≤30 mL/min.
    A reduced dose for moderate impairment does not make it suitable at CrCl 24.

    Discuss alternative pharmacological and psychosocial treatment with the
    addiction service, considering liver function, opioid use and other
    contraindications. Acamprosate maintains abstinence; it does not treat acute
    withdrawal.


    Reference: [Acamprosate: renal impairment](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dail
    ymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=5ebfa8c9-acba-b77e-e063-6294a90ab712).

--- [37] SLIDE: Read the case: key clues  (q-case-hd-clues)
Mohammed, 67, is on haemodialysis. He has low mood, poor sleep, poor appetite

--- [38] Q10  (q-case-hd) -------------------------------------------------------
Mohammed, 67, is on haemodialysis. He has low mood, poor sleep, poor appetite
and itch.

Which is the best initial medication option?

  A. Amitriptyline 25 mg at night
* B. Mirtazapine 7.5 mg at night
  C. Diazepam 5 mg at night
  D. Desvenlafaxine 100 mg daily

  ANSWER: B
  EXPLANATION:
    Low-dose mirtazapine helps sleep and appetite, may ease uraemic pruritus,
    and is a reasonable antidepressant on dialysis. Its clearance is reduced, so
    start at 7.5 mg and titrate slowly while watching for sedation and falls.

    - Amitriptyline: anticholinergic, orthostatic and QT effects.
    - Diazepam: active metabolites accumulate.
    - Desvenlafaxine: at most 50 mg every second day on dialysis.

    Also check dialysis adequacy, phosphate, PTH and iron. Itch has many
    treatable contributors.

--- [39] SLIDE: GP prescribing checklist  (checklist)
1. Know the current eGFR/CrCl and whether the patient is on dialysis
2. Is the drug renally cleared, does it accumulate, or is it nephrotoxic?
3. Start low, go slow. Review sedation, falls and cognition
4. Check Na, K, Mg and an ECG when combining QT-prolonging drugs
5. Lithium: level, eGFR, Ca and TSH every 3–6 months; give sick-day rules in
   writing
6. Transplant: check every new, stopped or changed drug, including herbal ones,
   for CYP3A4 effects. Tell the transplant team
7. New psychiatric symptoms on steroids or a calcineurin inhibitor: exclude
   organic causes first

--- [40] SLIDE: Take-home messages  (take-home)
- Sertraline (or low-dose mirtazapine) is a sensible start in advanced CKD;
  expect modest benefit
- Lithium: monitor, write down sick-day rules, and involve nephrology early
- Gabapentinoids cause encephalopathy and myoclonus in CKD: dose to CrCl
- Interactions: carbamazepine and St John's wort can lower tacrolimus levels;
  fluoxetine may increase levels. Coordinate changes with the transplant team
- Confusion, seizures, headache or visual change on tacrolimus is an emergency
