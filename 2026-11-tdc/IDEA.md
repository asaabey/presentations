Psychiatric pharmacotherapy in kidney disease and transplantation
Proposed GP education session | 40–45 minutes

I think this would be a very useful topic for GPs, particularly if you combine psychiatric medication prescribing in CKD with drug interactions in kidney transplant recipients.

My suggestion is to make it a case-based, clinically practical presentation, rather than a comprehensive review of psychopharmacology.
1. What should GPs be able to do after the presentation?
I would aim for five learning outcomes:
1. Select a reasonable first-line antidepressant in patients with CKD stages 3–5, including dialysis.
2. Identify psychotropic medications that accumulate in renal impairment and recognise their toxicity.
3. Understand lithium-associated kidney disease and safely manage lithium during acute illness.
4. Recognise important interactions between psychiatric medications and transplant immunosuppressants.
5. Differentiate primary psychiatric illness from uraemia, medication toxicity and immunosuppression-related neuropsychiatric complications.
The last two are particularly valuable because patients can present to general practice with anxiety, depression, tremor, insomnia, confusion or behavioural changes that aren't necessarily primary psychiatric disorders.

3. The medication tables GPs will actually use
A. Antidepressants in CKD
This is probably the most practically useful table in the entire presentation.
Medication	Renal considerations	Key GP message
Sertraline	Usually no formal renal dose adjustment	Often a useful first choice, including advanced CKD
Escitalopram	Caution in severe CKD	Consider QT risk, dose and electrolyte abnormalities
Fluoxetine	Generally little renal dose adjustment	Long half-life; important interaction potential
Venlafaxine	Reduced clearance of drug and metabolite	Dose reduction as kidney function declines
Desvenlafaxine	Significant renal clearance	Requires renal-specific dosing
Duloxetine	Exposure increases in severe impairment	Caution below CrCl 30; check Australian product-specific advice
Mirtazapine	Clearance may decrease	Start low; monitor sedation and falls

An important teaching nuance: Australian duloxetine product information is not completely uniform with international recommendations. Some Australian formulations describe 30 mg daily in severe renal impairment, whereas several renal psychiatry reviews advise avoiding it below CrCl 30 mL/min. This is worth teaching as a formulation- and guideline-specific prescribing decision, rather than a universal prohibition.

I would also emphasise that SSRIs are not free of renal-related problems: hyponatraemia, gastrointestinal bleeding risk with antiplatelets or anticoagulants, and QT prolongation can be clinically important.

A recent CKD medication safety curriculum specifically highlights lithium, pregabalin and gabapentin as important causes of drug accumulation and neurological toxicity.

The message should be that renal clearance, drug accumulation and nephrotoxicity are three different issues. A medicine may be non-nephrotoxic but still cause severe encephalopathy when kidney function declines.
4. The transplant drug-interaction section
This is where you can provide expertise beyond a standard GP prescribing lecture.
A. Important interactions with tacrolimus and ciclosporin
Psychiatric medication	Potential interaction	Practical action
Fluvoxamine	Enzyme inhibition may significantly increase calcineurin inhibitor exposure	Avoid routine co-prescribing without specialist oversight
Fluoxetine	Potential to increase immunosuppressant exposure	Check interactions and arrange level monitoring
Sertraline	Generally lower interaction potential	Often a reasonable option; still review interactions

Teach GPs to contact the transplant team when starting, stopping or changing a clinically interacting psychotropic medication. Tacrolimus trough levels and renal function may need additional monitoring; the interaction can reverse when the offending medicine is discontinued.
B. Is this really a psychiatric illness?
A particularly interesting teaching case would involve a kidney transplant recipient presenting with insomnia, agitation, tremor and altered behaviour.

The differential includes steroid-related mania or psychosis, tacrolimus neurotoxicity, infection, metabolic disturbance and primary psychiatric illness.

Tacrolimus can cause delirium, seizures and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES), as well as milder symptoms such as tremor and insomnia. 
Medicines Australia+1


I would emphasise that acute confusion, seizures, severe headache or visual disturbance require urgent assessment, not simply an antipsychotic prescription. Immunosuppression changes must be coordinated with the transplant service.
